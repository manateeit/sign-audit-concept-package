# 1. Executive summary

Hospitals, campuses, and other large buildings currently maintain sign inventories and compliance records through a manual, file-based process: a person walks the building photographing signs, manually matches photos to floor-plan locations, and a compliance specialist manually checks each sign against ADA, building/fire code, and life-safety requirements. **[FACT]** No vendor or trade source documents a standardized productivity benchmark for this photo-to-floor-plan matching step, which is itself evidence that the step is ad hoc, consultant-hour-intensive, and unmeasured across the industry (12pointsignworks.com, accessed 2026-08-04). **[FACT]** Inventories built this way decay between audit cycles rather than staying current — a pattern visible in the adjacent public-sector market, where a competing product's own marketing states "70% of municipalities have no ADA transition plan, or one that is decades out of date" (bluedag.com, accessed 2026-08-04). **[RECOMMENDATION]** The opportunity is to replace file-based, point-in-time surveys with a persistent, versioned, evidence-linked digital record of a building's signage — reducing consultant/specialist labor on the least-benchmarked step (organization and matching), and giving building owners a living asset instead of a stale PDF.

**Recommended path.** No published vendor or reference architecture does end-to-end "walk-and-scan sign compliance auditing" today **[ASSUMPTION, per architecture and competitive-landscape research]** — this is a greenfield category, not an adaptation of an existing product, and several capabilities central to a fully AI-driven version (sign-specific detection dataset, cross-visit deduplication, room-level indoor localization, hospital-lighting OCR accuracy) are unvalidated in the published literature. **[RECOMMENDATION]** The defensible path is therefore staged, not a single big-bang platform: (1) start by selling to third-party ADA/accessibility compliance consulting firms — the party already performing this exact manual chain as billable-hour labor, positioned to distribute the tool across their existing client rosters, and able to absorb AI output as decision support behind their own professional judgment rather than as an autonomous compliance determination; (2) validate the organizational/workflow layer (photo-to-location tagging, duplicate flagging, OCR-assisted search, structured reporting) before adding any unproven automation; (3) layer in AI-assisted detection, OCR/VLM classification, and localization — always producing *Findings* for mandatory human review, never autonomous verdicts — once pilot data validates detection recall, dedup accuracy, and room-assignment accuracy on real hospital corridors; (4) reserve LiDAR/photogrammetry/BIM-grade spatial-twin capture as a premium, campus-scale option for customers who will pay for the reusable 3D asset (space planning, emergency response, renovation tracking) beyond compliance alone, since that capture mode is the slowest and most expensive to deploy.

**Key risks.** A note on labeling in this subsection: the claims below are compressed for the executive summary; each is re-stated, cited, and tagged in full in its originating section (Sections 2, 4, 5, 10, and 11) — the tags here mark the same epistemic status, just at summary length.

- *Technical.* **[FACT, CV-feasibility lane]** Sign detection, cross-visit deduplication, indoor localization to room/door granularity, and hospital-lighting OCR accuracy each lack a published, domain-specific validation (no purpose-built hospital-signage benchmark exists) and are documented, in adjacent domains, to drift or fail under specific conditions (long repetitive corridors, elevators, oblique/stylized text). **[ASSUMPTION]** None of this is proven off-the-shelf technology for signage specifically, even though the component techniques (object detection, OCR, VIO/SLAM) are individually mature elsewhere.
- *Regulatory/liability.* **[FACT]** The applicable rule stack is genuinely tiered — binding federal ADA law, non-binding-until-adopted model codes, state amendments, local AHJ discretion, and a separate accreditation track (Joint Commission) with a different signage focus (life-safety/egress, not ADA room-ID). **[RECOMMENDATION]** Any system that flattens these tiers into a single automated pass/fail risks producing findings that cannot survive AHJ, surveyor, or litigation scrutiny.
- *Privacy.* **[FACT]** Any camera-based capture inside a hospital carries incidental PHI exposure risk (whiteboards, monitors, wristbands, bystanders). **[FACT]** A documented $2.2M HHS OCR settlement against a hospital for uncontrolled filming access is a direct, binding-enforcement precedent for what happens when this risk is not controlled (hhs.gov, 2016, accessed 2026-08-04).
- **[RECOMMENDATION]** Across all three risk categories, the mitigating design constraint is the same: keep AI output as decision support requiring a qualified human reviewer's sign-off (see Section 10 for proposed minimum reviewer-qualification criteria), restrict raw-media access, and route physically unmeasurable characteristics (mounting height without scale reference, LRV contrast, tactile/Braille dimensions) to explicit "requires field verification" status rather than auto-closing them.

# 2. Problem and opportunity definition

## 2.1 The problem, grounded in current practice

The existing sign-compliance audit cycle is triggered by new construction/certificate of occupancy, renovation or department relocation, a periodic re-audit tied to an accreditation cycle, a complaint/demand letter, or a scheduled ADA self-evaluation refresh **[ASSUMPTION, consistent with general ADA/FM literature]**. Once triggered, the cycle runs through nine largely manual, file-based steps — scoping, pre-survey prep, field survey, data consolidation, compliance scoring, report/remediation planning, remediation execution, verification/closeout, and decay until the next trigger (full detail in Section 3.1). **[FACT]** Every handoff between these steps is file-based — PDF, Excel, email — with no shared system of record, which is precisely the kind of point where version drift or information loss occurs (inferred from tooling described by 12pointsignworks.com and BlueDAG's competitive positioning, accessed 2026-08-04).

Three quantified pain points anchor the size of the problem: sign fabrication and installation runs **$45–$250+ per sign** (blinksigns.com, accessed 2026-08-04); a full wayfinding/signage system overhaul for a major hospital runs **$150,000–$500,000+** (nicolsonassociates.com, accessed 2026-08-04); and poor wayfinding alone has been documented to cost one hospital system roughly **4,500 staff-hours per year** in staff giving directions (Emory University Hospital case study, cited via eyedog.mobi, accessed 2026-08-04). **[ESTIMATE]** No hard figure exists for the total number of signs in a typical hospital, or a labor-hours-per-sign benchmark for the field-survey/photo-matching step — this absence is itself notable evidence that the step is unbenchmarked in current practice, and it is flagged as an open question rather than assumed away.

## 2.2 Bottlenecks and failure modes

**[ASSUMPTION]** The primary bottleneck is manual photo-to-floor-plan matching — unbenchmarked, consultant-hour-intensive, and the step most exposed to transcription error. A second bottleneck is tracking remediation to closure across facilities/vendor/contractor handoffs with no shared system of record. A third, structural failure mode is that inventories go stale between audit cycles rather than being maintained continuously; the next trigger event typically forces a full re-survey rather than an incremental update, especially after staff or consultant turnover — a documented general facilities-management risk (fmj.ifma.org, accessed 2026-08-04), though no hospital-signage-specific case study quantifying this rate was located **[OPEN QUESTION]**. Likewise, a targeted search for hospital-specific ADA *signage* litigation or settlements returned only adjacent sectors (hotels, retail, athletic clubs) — this is an open question, not evidence of absence.

## 2.3 Stakeholders and the recommended entry segment

Six stakeholder roles recur across the current process: the Facilities Director/VP of Facilities (owns the remediation budget); the ADA/Accessibility Coordinator (required for Title II public entities, common voluntarily in private Title III hospitals); Risk Management/Legal-Compliance (reviews findings for litigation exposure); the Life Safety Officer/Joint Commission survey-prep team (owns a *different* signage concern — life-safety/egress under LS.02.01.20 — from a different internal seat than the ADA coordinator **[FACT]**); third-party ADA/accessibility consultants (general consultants, CASp in California, RAS in Texas) who perform the survey when in-house bandwidth is lacking; and architects/sign vendors/GCs who execute remediation.

**[RECOMMENDATION]** The strongest initial customer segment is third-party ADA/accessibility compliance consulting firms, not a single large hospital system. These firms already perform the exact manual chain (survey → photo/floor-plan matching → report → punch list) as billable-hour labor, feeling the pain directly rather than secondhand; selling to one firm unlocks distribution across its entire client roster rather than a single-logo enterprise sale; a tool that augments a paid human expert's judgment is a lower trust/liability bar than a hospital adopting an AI system directly into its own compliance chain; and it avoids, for a first deployment, the heaviest procurement gauntlet (hospital IT security review, HIPAA BAAs, escorted access to restricted units) since the consultant already holds facility-access agreements with their hospital clients. A single large hospital system optimizing for Joint Commission prep is the *weakest* first segment, because TJC's signage interest is narrowly life-safety/egress — a different rule set and buyer than ADA room-signage — while the enterprise sales cycle and infrequent survey cadence (~every 3 years) make it a slow first logo. **[RECOMMENDATION]** A second-priority segment is a multi-site outpatient/ambulatory portfolio under one health system's real-estate/facilities group — faster internal procurement, fewer HIPAA-restricted units, and high documented ADA-litigation exposure in commercial/retail contexts creates budget urgency.

# 3. Current-state and future-state workflows

## 3.1 Current-state workflow (manual process, step by step)

1. **Scoping/kickoff** — Facilities Director or ADA/Accessibility Coordinator defines building/floor/sign-category scope and decides in-house vs. third-party consultant; on-site audit is the norm for large/complex facilities over a desk audit **[FACT]** (12pointsignworks.com; clinician.com).
2. **Pre-survey prep** — existing floor plans, prior sign schedules, and room lists are pulled, often from a CAFM/CMMS system **[ASSUMPTION]**.
3. **Field survey** — a person walks door-to-door photographing each sign and recording mounting height, character/Braille compliance, contrast/finish, and placement against a checklist **[FACT]** (12pointsignworks.com).
4. **Data consolidation** — photos and field notes are manually matched to floor-plan locations and room numbers, typically in a spreadsheet; this matching is largely ad hoc and unbenchmarked in current practice **[ASSUMPTION/OPEN QUESTION]**.
5. **Compliance scoring** — each sign is checked against the applicable stack (ADA 2010 Standards §703, ICC/IBC, NFPA 101/ANSI A117.1 as locally adopted, state/local amendments), producing a compliance score and a prioritized deficiency list, life-safety/egress items typically ranked highest **[FACT]** (12pointsignworks.com).
6. **Report and remediation plan** — a PDF audit report plus a phased remediation plan is delivered; for Title II-covered or quasi-public hospitals this may formally become part of an ADA transition plan **[FACT]** (12pointsignworks.com; adata.org).
7. **Remediation execution** — facilities issues work orders to a sign vendor/fabricator (and a GC if structural work is needed near egress paths); fabrication/install runs $45–$250+ per sign **[FACT]** (blinksigns.com).
8. **Verification/closeout** — re-inspection confirms the fix and, ideally, the inventory record is updated.
9. **Decay and reinspection** — in practice the inventory is rarely kept current between cycles; the next trigger (renovation, accreditation cycle, complaint) tends to force a full re-survey rather than an incremental update **[ASSUMPTION]**.

**Artifacts produced:** raw photo sets, a marked-up floor plan, an Excel sign schedule, a PDF audit/deficiency report, a prioritized punch list, work orders, installation proof photos, and (for covered entities) a formal transition-plan document — all connected by file-based, non-versioned handoffs.

## 3.2 Future-state workflow (generic — concept-specific detail follows in the recommended concept)

**[RECOMMENDATION]** Regardless of which concept is ultimately selected, the future state replaces the manual chain above with an AI-assisted capture-to-remediation workflow structured as follows. The *degree* of automation at each step, and the capture hardware required, is concept-specific and is addressed elsewhere in this package (see Concepts A/B/C); the sequence itself generalizes across all three:

1. **Scoping** — scope defined digitally (building/floor/zone) with an existing floor plan (PDF/CAD/IFC) imported or digitized in-app, rather than marked up on paper.
2. **Capture** — an operator walks escort-scoped zones capturing photo, video, and/or spatial data (device and continuous-vs-stop-and-shoot mode vary by concept); location is bound to the floor plan through some combination of operator confirmation, opportunistic placard/QR reading, and device motion tracking — never GPS, which does not function reliably indoors **[FACT]**.
3. **Local processing/QC** — on-device or near-real-time image-quality checks (blur, glare, framing) prompt retakes before the operator moves on, and local buffering ensures no data loss in the hospital's documented Wi-Fi/cellular dead zones **[FACT]**.
4. **Async enrichment** — once connectivity returns, heavier processing runs: sign candidate detection (where automated), duplicate/candidate-merge flagging, OCR/text and pictogram extraction, and location reconciliation against the floor plan.
5. **Compliance evaluation** — each sign-at-a-location is checked against the jurisdiction-resolved rule stack applicable to that building, producing structured *Findings* (not verdicts) with a cited rule version and confidence indicator where AI assistance was used.
6. **Human review** — a qualified reviewer sees the evidence, the applicable rule text, and any AI-proposed finding side by side, and concurs, overrides, or marks the item "requires field verification." This step is present and load-bearing in every concept under evaluation, per the project's decision-support boundary.
7. **Report/export and remediation tracking** — approved findings generate the audit report/punch list automatically and, where useful, push structured work orders into the customer's existing CMMS rather than duplicating it.
8. **Re-audit** — subsequent visits are scoped, targeted revisits (flagged gaps or remediation-pending signs) whose new evidence is version-linked to the same sign record — supersede, not duplicate — preserving a continuous audit trail across the life of the building rather than restarting from zero each cycle.

The structural improvement over the current state is not any single automated judgment but the elimination of file-based, non-versioned handoffs: evidence, location, rule citation, AI output, and reviewer decision live in one linked, append-only record instead of being re-assembled by hand at every step.

# 4. Regulatory landscape and automation boundaries

## 4.1 Authority tiers governing hospital signage

| Tier | Examples | Binding? | Jurisdictional variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Federal ADA law | 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design §703 (and §216 scoping), under Title II (public entities) and Title III (private entities) | **[FACT]** Binding federal civil-rights law, enforced by DOJ and by private right of action; no local adoption needed (ada.gov) | None — uniform nationwide floor, subject to a practitioner-understood "safe harbor" for unaltered pre-1991-ADAAG elements **[ASSUMPTION]** |
| Federal accessibility guidelines | US Access Board ADA/ABA Accessibility Guidelines | **[FACT]** Not directly enforceable on their own — binding only once adopted into an enforceable standard (e.g., the 2010 ADA Standards) (access-board.gov) | Low — mainly federally-funded/federal-agency facilities |
| Model consensus standard | ANSI A117.1 | **[FACT]** Not law on its own; binding only when a state/local building code references it | Moderate — edition adopted (2009/2017/2020) varies by state |
| Model building/fire code | ICC/IBC, IFC (e.g., IBC §1013 exit signs) | **[FACT]** Not law until a state/local jurisdiction adopts it, often with amendments | High — edition and local amendments vary widely |
| Model life-safety code | NFPA 101 Life Safety Code | **[FACT]** Non-binding alone, but made binding for Medicare/Medicaid-participating hospitals via CMS Conditions of Participation (currently referencing the 2012 edition, plus NFPA 99) (cms.gov) | Moderate — CMS edition is nationally uniform for CMS-regulated hospitals, but states may separately adopt a different edition for licensing, creating dual-standard exposure |
| State law | State building/fire codes and amendments, state hospital licensing rules | **[FACT]** Binding, supersedes/modifies model codes; often cross-references NFPA/IBC editions independently of CMS's chosen edition | High |
| Local AHJ | Local ordinances and AHJ interpretation | **[FACT]** Binding locally; AHJ retains interpretive/enforcement discretion | Highest — can differ block to block |
| Accreditation | Joint Commission EC/LS chapters (LS.02.01.20 means-of-egress) | **[FACT]** Not government law, but a CMS "deemed status" pathway; non-compliance risks accreditation loss, which can trigger loss of CMS deemed status (jointcommission.org) | Low variance in text; survey rigor varies by surveyor |
| Federal regulation | CMS Conditions of Participation (42 CFR 482), including EMTALA posting mandates | **[FACT]** Binding on any hospital billing Medicare/Medicaid — nearly all US hospitals (cms.gov, QSO-24-17) | Low federally, but "conspicuous placement" language leaves siting judgment to surveyors |

**The critical distinction for a rules engine:** ADA room-identification signage and Joint Commission/CMS life-safety egress signage are two different rule sets with two different internal owners inside a hospital (the ADA Coordinator vs. the Life Safety Officer) **[FACT]**, and a system that presents both under one undifferentiated "compliance score" risks obscuring which authority tier — and which internal stakeholder — a given finding actually belongs to. **[RECOMMENDATION]** Every rule record should carry its authority tier, jurisdiction scope, and adopted-edition metadata explicitly, and every finding should be traceable to the exact rule version in force on the audit date, so a re-audit after a code change can diff "what applied then" against "what applies now."

## 4.2 What is safe to automate vs. what must stay human-decided

Per the project's governing boundary, AI compliance output is decision support for a qualified human reviewer unless research shows a specific, narrow decision can be safely and legally fully automated. Applying the CV-feasibility and field-usability research to that boundary yields three tiers:

**Safe for full automation without per-instance human review.** A narrow class of *completeness/QC* judgments, not legal or compliance determinations: blur/glare/exposure retake prompts, best-frame selection, and coverage/missed-area flagging (a heatmap of captured camera positions against the floor plan, or a room/door-count proxy against a room list). **[RECOMMENDATION]** These qualify because false positives cost a technician only a brief extra look, false negatives are self-correcting at the next audit cycle, and none of them constitute a legal or code determination about a sign's compliance. This is the one class of decision in the research corpus that meets the "safe to fully automate" bar the project's boundary requires — everything else below stays gated behind human review.

**Automatable to a draft/candidate stage, requiring mandatory human sign-off before being authoritative.** Sign detection, duplicate/candidate-merge flagging, OCR/VLM text and pictogram classification, floor-plan/room location assignment, and rule-matching against measurable, photo-derivable criteria (character style/case/font family, presence of a pictogram field, general sign layout, presence-only checks such as whether an EMTALA posting exists at all). **[FACT/RECOMMENDATION]** No evidence surfaced in this research that any AHJ or accreditor accepts a fully automated sign-compliance determination today, so every one of these outputs must be presented as a *Finding* — evidence, rule citation, AI confidence — for a reviewer to concur with, override, or escalate, never as a silent, final answer.

**Not currently automatable to compliance-grade confidence from ordinary photo/video, regardless of review posture.** Mounting height (without a scale reference, LiDAR, or SfM pipeline); visual contrast/LRV — the Access Board's guidance on ADA signage contrast is commonly operationalized by accessibility consultants as a minimum ~70% Light Reflectance Value (LRV) difference between characters/background, and industry sign-fabrication sources typically frame the practical target as a ~65–70% LRV-difference threshold **[FACT/ASSUMPTION, secondary-sourced via signs.org and latitudesignage.com — see Section 19; not independently verified by this research against primary 36 CFR Part 1191 text, and flagged again as an open verification item in Section 16]** — a physical colorimeter reading is the industry practice specifically because camera auto-exposure/white-balance/compression distort reflectance; tactile character depth and Braille dot geometry (LiDAR mean deviation ~5mm vs. the ADA's ~0.8mm raised-character minimum — an order of magnitude too coarse); and any judgment call resting on AHJ discretion or "conspicuous placement"/sightline reasoning. **[FACT, CV-feasibility lane]** **[RECOMMENDATION]** Findings touching these characteristics should be routed "requires field verification" rather than auto-closed in either direction — closing them on AI inference alone would produce a record a surveyor or opposing counsel could characterize as unsupported.

## 4.3 Privacy as a cross-cutting automation boundary

**[FACT]** HIPAA's Privacy Rule generally tolerates incidental PHI capture in public/hallway areas where reasonable safeguards apply, but restricted units — behavioral health, NICU, OR, ED, pharmacy/security — warrant an explicit capture-exclusion or heightened-authorization policy regardless of automation level, and the New York-Presbyterian $2.2M HHS OCR settlement for unfettered filming access is a direct, binding-enforcement-backed precedent for what happens when this is not controlled (hhs.gov, 2016). **[RECOMMENDATION]** No automation decision in this package overrides that constraint: capture-exclusion zones, on-device redaction before cloud transmission, and RBAC-gated raw-media access are required controls independent of which compliance-automation tier a given finding falls into.

# 5. Technology feasibility matrix

| Capability | Feasibility | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| Sign detection / localization in frame | Feasible today | Mature general-purpose object detectors (YOLOv12, RF-DETR-class transformers) are commercially available; no purpose-built hospital-signage benchmark exists publicly, so a custom-labeled training set is required — a data/engineering task, not unproven research **[FACT/ASSUMPTION]**. |
| OCR of clear, printed sign text | Feasible today | Printed-text OCR reaches ~98–99% accuracy under controlled conditions (PaddleOCR/EasyOCR/Tesseract all scored 0.85–0.93 confidence in a 2025 comparison) **[FACT]**. |
| OCR of stylized/oblique/low-contrast scene text | Feasible with added hardware or procedure | Accuracy drops to ~62–80% word accuracy on complex/stylized fonts; VLM-assisted OCR plus mandatory human spot-check is needed to treat output as a searchable hint, not a compliance record **[FACT]**. |
| Pictogram/symbol recognition (ISA wheelchair, exit, biohazard, etc.) | Feasible today | Closed-set image classification is a simpler problem than open scene text and standard CNN/ViT classifiers should perform well, though no benchmark specifically validates this for ADA/ISO 7001-style symbols — the closest adjacent work is a different scale/context **[ASSUMPTION]**. |
| Best-frame selection (blur/glare/exposure scoring) | Feasible today | Laplacian-variance blur detection and Sobel-filter glare thresholds are established, computationally cheap techniques already used in production keyframe selection **[FACT]**. |
| Coverage/missed-area heatmap (camera-position proxy) | Feasible today | Logging camera position/orientation against the floor-plan trace is standard practice in construction 360°-capture tools already **[FACT]**. |
| True semantic "was this room skipped" coverage inference | Requires further R&D | Next-best-view/coverage-planning for arbitrary building interiors is an active academic research area without a mature off-the-shelf solution; a room/door-count proxy is a workable substitute pending field validation **[FACT]**. |
| Duplicate suppression across a single walkthrough | Requires further R&D | No paper specifically addresses "duplicate sign suppression in a building walkthrough"; mapping it to SLAM/place-recognition landmark re-observation is a reasoned inference, not a cited validated result **[ASSUMPTION]**. |
| Change detection across repeat surveys (moved/replaced/damaged sign) | Requires further R&D | No dedicated literature located; conceptually adjacent to construction-progress and remote-sensing change detection but unvalidated for indoor repeat-visit sign inventories **[FACT]**. |
| Mounting-height measurement | Feasible with added hardware or procedure | Ordinary photo without a scale reference cannot yield inches; LiDAR (iPad Pro, ~1–2cm accuracy, ~5m range) or an in-frame calibration card/SfM pipeline recovers metric scale **[FACT]**. |
| Visual contrast (LRV) measurement | Not currently feasible from photo/video | Industry practice measures LRV with a physical colorimeter directly on the sign, targeting the ADA-guidance-derived minimum ~70% contrast between characters and background (commonly operationalized by consultants as a ~65–70% LRV-difference threshold — see Section 4.2 for sourcing and its unverified-against-primary-text caveat), because camera auto-exposure, white balance, and compression distort true reflectance **[FACT/ASSUMPTION, see Section 4.2 and Section 16 for the specific-figure caveat]**. |
| Non-glare finish / gloss level (quantitative pass/fail) | Not currently feasible | Qualitative specular-glare flagging is visible in a photo, but pass/fail against a numeric gloss-level spec needs a glossmeter or manufacturer cut sheet **[FACT]**. |
| Tactile (raised) character depth | Not currently feasible | ADA requires raised characters ≥1/32 in. (~0.8mm); even close-range LiDAR mean deviation is ~5mm, an order of magnitude coarser than the feature being measured **[FACT]**. |
| Braille presence (is there a Braille cell at all) | Feasible with added hardware or procedure | Detectable from close, well-lit, near-perpendicular shots, but not from oblique hallway-walkthrough angles **[ASSUMPTION]**. |
| Braille correctness (dot spacing/size to spec) | Not currently feasible | The strongest published CV result (mAP50 94.8, ICCV2021W) was trained on flat, close-range, controlled-lighting images — a large domain gap from real-world walkthrough footage **[FACT]**. |
| UL 924 listing / label verification | Feasible with added hardware or procedure | A label may be legible in a close photo, but confirming listing/adequacy requires pairing the image with manufacturer documentation, not vision alone **[ASSUMPTION]**. |
| Illumination level (fc/lux) and 90-min emergency-power duration for egress signs | Not currently feasible from video | Instrument-based (light meter) and time-based testing are required; not derivable from a single photo or video clip **[FACT]**. |
| Indoor relative motion tracking (VIO/SLAM, single segment) | Feasible today | ARKit-class visual-inertial odometry achieves ~0.19m drift error over a tracking session with ~0.02 m/s relative drift — solid for short-range relative tracking **[FACT]**. |
| Indoor localization at room/door granularity across floors and elevators | Feasible with added hardware or procedure | VIO alone drifts and elevators break vision-only tracking via non-inertial motion; a layered VIO + QR/AprilTag or OCR'd-placard checkpoint + human floor-plan confirmation approach is currently the only defensible method — no single automated method is independently trustworthy at this granularity **[FACT/RECOMMENDATION]**. |
| GPS-based indoor positioning | Not currently feasible | Walls/roofs/floor slabs attenuate GNSS signals below the acquisition/tracking noise floor and multipath reflection produces large, unstable errors rather than graceful degradation; this is a hard architectural exclusion, not a tuning problem **[FACT]**. |
| Metric-scale spatial capture via LiDAR/photogrammetry (digital-twin tier) | Feasible with added hardware or procedure | iPad Pro LiDAR: ~1–2cm accuracy but only ~5m effective range; photogrammetry/SfM achieved 0.007m systematic error in a controlled study but requires sufficient parallax/coverage and non-real-time post-processing **[FACT]**. |
| BIM/IFC model alignment as a coordinate reference | Feasible with added hardware or procedure, contingent on asset quality | IFC (ISO 16739) is the vendor-neutral schema for room/floor topology, but accurate as-built BIM is rare outside recent new construction, and registration errors compound silently into every downstream location/measurement unless a dedicated alignment-review gate is added **[FACT/RECOMMENDATION]**. |

**Note for pilot design.** Several rows above are marked "Requires further R&D" or carry no published domain benchmark (sign-specific detection accuracy under hospital lighting, dedup precision/recall, room-assignment accuracy, OCR misread rates on real placards). These are not settled by the literature reviewed in this package and must be measured directly in a pilot before any commercial commitment: sign-detection recall/precision, duplicate-detection accuracy, OCR/classification accuracy, indoor-location accuracy and percentage of signs correctly assigned to a room/doorway, capture time per floor or square foot, human review time saved, missed-sign rate, false-compliance-finding rate, percentage of compliance attributes requiring manual measurement, and successful change detection across repeat surveys are the specific metrics this feasibility matrix leaves open and that the pilot plan(s) elsewhere in this package are designed to close.

# 6. Comparison of the three solution concepts

| Dimension | Concept A — Low-Risk Assisted Audit | Concept B — AI-Assisted Inventory & Compliance Platform | Concept C — Spatial Digital Twin |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Automation level** | Low. Human-tagged location, human-identified signs, human compliance judgment throughout. Only assistive flags (blur/glare, OCR-as-search-hint, duplicate-candidate flags) are automated, all requiring human confirmation. | Medium-high. Detection, dedup, OCR/VLM classification, room/location assignment, and rule-matching on photo-derivable criteria run automatically to a *draft* stage; mandatory human sign-off before any finding is authoritative. Blur/glare/coverage-gap QC is fully automated end-to-end (no human loop needed). | Same automated draft-finding layer as B, **plus** genuine automation of metric measurement (mounting height, viewing-distance scaling) from point-cloud/LiDAR geometry — a capability A and B cannot offer. Tactile depth, Braille geometry, and LRV remain unautomatable in all three concepts. [FACT, CV-feasibility lane] |

**Direct comparison.** Concept A and Concept B sit at opposite ends of a single automation continuum applied to the *same* underlying customer segment and workflow (third-party ADA consulting firms; escort-zone capture; human-reviewed findings) — B is not a different product, it is A plus a detection/dedup/OCR/rules-engine layer inserted between capture and review. Concept C is a different kind of bet — it targets a different primary buyer (Facilities/Real Estate leadership valuing the *spatial asset*, not the ADA consulting workflow), requires specialist hardware and crews the other two concepts don't need, and its central claimed advantage (automating metric measurement) only matters for a narrow slice of characteristics (mounting height, character-size-at-distance) — it does nothing for the CV-feasibility lane's harder unsolved problems (tactile depth, Braille dimensions, LRV contrast), which remain manual regardless of capture fidelity. C is therefore best understood as a premium capture-hardware upgrade path layered onto B's software stack for customers who want the twin for its own sake, not a standalone alternative to A or B for the ADA-consulting-firm buyer this research identified as the strongest initial segment [RECOMMENDATION, business-process lane].

# 7. Recommended initial product and rationale

**Recommendation: build Concept A first, sold to third-party ADA/accessibility compliance consulting firms as both customer and distribution channel, with an explicit, pre-committed roadmap to evolve it into Concept B once A's riskiest behavioral assumption is validated in a real building. Concept C should not be built at all as a near-term product — it should be held as a possible premium upsell (or acquisition of/partnership with an existing reality-capture vendor's output) for the minority of customers who value the spatial-twin asset independent of compliance.** [RECOMMENDATION]

Rationale, weighed directly against the alternatives:

1. **A is the only concept with zero unproven technical dependencies.** Every capability A requires — offline-first mobile capture, tap-on-floor-plan location tagging, blur/glare scoring, cloud OCR on clean text, timestamp/location duplicate-flagging — is established, commercially available technology today [FACT, CV-feasibility and capture-workflow lanes]. B and C both depend on capabilities the research corpus explicitly flags as unvalidated R&D for this specific domain: no purpose-built building-signage detection dataset exists publicly, no literature validates sign-specific dedup-as-landmark-matching or change-detection across repeat indoor surveys, and no benchmark exists for detection/OCR accuracy under real hospital lighting/glare [FACT/ASSUMPTION, CV-feasibility lane]. Committing to B or C's full build before that R&D risk is retired would be spending $1.2M-$3M+ against assumptions no one has field-tested.
2. **A matches how the strongest initial customer segment already buys.** **[RECOMMENDATION, business-process lane]** The business-process research lane recommends third-party ADA consulting firms as the first customer specifically because they feel the manual photo-to-floor-plan-matching pain directly as billable-hour labor, and because selling to one firm unlocks distribution across its whole client roster. **[FACT, business-process lane]** That same lane's finding that "one-time project audits" are the current market norm maps exactly onto A's per-project/seat commercial model. **[RECOMMENDATION]** A can therefore be sold into the existing buying motion with no category-creation burden, whereas B and C ask the buyer to trust automated AI findings before any deployed reference exists anywhere in this market. **[FACT, competitive-landscape lane]** The closest analog, Reconstruct's Automated Inspector, is asset/equipment-centric and is not shown to specialize in signage — **[ASSUMPTION]** a gap this research infers A can occupy without first proving out a detector, though no direct head-to-head evidence against Reconstruct was available to confirm this.
3. **A is the lowest-liability way to enter a compliance-adjacent, healthcare-adjacent market.** **[FACT]** Every concept carries HIPAA/PHI and restricted-unit exposure common to any camera-based capture inside a hospital (per the NY-Presbyterian $2.2M OCR settlement precedent discussed in Sections 1, 4.3, and 11). **[ASSUMPTION]** B and C add a *second* liability axis A does not have: an AI-generated compliance recommendation that a human reviewer might over-trust, or that a plaintiff/AHJ might later scrutinize as unsupported inference. **[RECOMMENDATION]** Shipping A first lets the product earn trust and real-world evidence before that second liability axis is introduced at all.
4. **A directly produces the training data and design-partner relationship B needs.** **[RECOMMENDATION]** A's output — organized, location-tagged photo sets with a compliance specialist's finalized judgment attached to each — is exactly the labeled dataset a custom sign detector and rules engine need, and A's paying consulting-firm customers become natural design partners for a B-stage pilot. **[RECOMMENDATION]** This is the single strongest argument in the corpus for sequencing rather than parallel-building B or C: Concept A's own analysis states this explicitly ("use the resulting evidence corpus... as training data... that a pure-software competitor entering later would lack").
5. **B's own kill/pivot criteria and A's own pilot criteria compose into one staged decision gate.** **[FACT]** A's validating result (≥30% specialist review-time reduction, <5% location-tagging error) is the evidence needed to justify funding B's harder build; B's validating result (≥90% detection recall, <5% dedup error, ≥80% correct room assignment, ≥40% reviewer-time reduction) becomes the gate for whether AI-proposed findings are trustworthy enough to layer onto A's now-proven review UI. **[RECOMMENDATION]** Building A first turns these into sequential, de-risked checkpoints instead of a single all-or-nothing 24-month bet.
6. **C is excluded from the near-term roadmap, not merely deprioritized**, because its primary buyer (Facilities/Real Estate leadership valuing the spatial asset for space-planning/emergency-response reuse) is a different sale, with a different value proposition, from the ADA-consulting-firm segment this recommendation targets — and because its unique technical benefit (metric measurement automation) addresses only mounting-height/character-size checks, a narrow slice of the compliance stack, while its cost and privacy exposure are categorically higher. If a future customer specifically wants the reusable digital twin, the recommended path is to integrate with or resell an existing capture vendor's (Matterport/NavVis-class) output rather than build specialist-hardware capture in-house [RECOMMENDATION, commercial-model lane].

**Staged path, stated plainly:** Ship Concept A → run its pilot (2-3 consulting-firm technicians, parallel manual-vs-tool audits, measuring review-time savings and tagging error rate) → if validated, begin layering in B's detection/dedup/OCR/rules-engine components incrementally onto A's proven evidence-store-and-review-UI shell, gated by B's own pilot criteria → treat C as an optional, separately-priced capture-hardware upgrade offered only if a customer's stated need is the spatial asset itself, not signage compliance.

# 8. Proposed architecture and data model

The architecture below is the full target-state system (drawing on the product-architecture research lane), annotated by build phase so it reads honestly against the staged recommendation in Section 7: **[Phase 1]** = built for the Concept A launch, **[Phase 2]** = added when evolving toward Concept B, **[Phase 3 / optional]** = the Concept C spatial-twin layer, built only on customer-specific demand.

## Major components

- **Media Ingestion** *[Phase 1]* — mobile capture app with offline-first local buffering (SQLite/on-device DB as source of truth during capture) and resumable upload once connectivity returns; required from day one because hospital Wi-Fi/cellular dead zones are documented, not hypothetical [FACT, capture-workflow lane].
- **Manual/Assisted Location Tagging** *[Phase 1]* — tap-on-floor-plan and room-placard confirmation UI; binds each photo to a stable location key without any automated positioning, deliberately avoiding VIO/SLAM drift and elevator-tracking failure modes documented in the indoor-localization lane.
- **Evidence Store** *[Phase 1]* — versioned repository of photo + location + OCR text + specialist notes per audit cycle; the canonical system of record A sells on.
- **Basic Processing** *[Phase 1]* — blur/glare scoring (Laplacian variance/Sobel-filter thresholds), cloud OCR for text-as-search-index (not a compliance record), and timestamp/location-proximity duplicate-candidate flagging for human confirmation.
- **Human Review UI** *[Phase 1, extended in Phase 2]* — floor-plan-indexed gallery with rule-citation reference text side by side; in Phase 1 the specialist enters findings manually, in Phase 2 the same UI additionally displays AI-proposed findings, confidence scores, and the specific rule threshold compared against.
- **Reporting/Export** *[Phase 1]* — auto-generates the audit report/punch-list/floor-plan overlay from entered findings, eliminating manual photo-to-spreadsheet-to-report transcription.
- **Detection Pipeline** *[Phase 2]* — frame extraction → sign detector (custom-labeled or open-vocabulary model) → best-frame selection (sharpness/glare/angle scoring) → dedup against nearby frames and prior visits → embedding generation for similarity search.
- **OCR/VLM Classification** *[Phase 2]* — reads/classifies sign text, pictograms, color, material, mount type; VLM-assisted OCR preferred over classical OCR for stylized/oblique scene text per the CV-feasibility lane's accuracy findings.
- **Indoor Localization Fusion** *[Phase 2]* — VIO/SLAM (ARKit-class) fused with OCR'd-placard/QR checkpoint corrections, replacing Phase 1's manual-only tagging with drift-corrected automated positioning; Phase 1's tap-confirmation UI is retained as the human reconciliation layer, not discarded.
- **Compliance Rules Engine** *[Phase 2]* — jurisdiction resolver mapping address + facility type + CMS provider number to the applicable federal/model-code/state/local/accreditation rule stack, each rule independently versioned; emits **Findings**, never verdicts.
- **Remediation/Work-Order Tracking** *[Phase 2]* — ticket lifecycle, with export into the customer's existing CMMS rather than duplicating it.
- **Spatial/Digital-Twin Layer** *[Phase 3, optional]* — persistent 3D coordinate frame (point cloud + BIM alignment) that `SignObservation` and other-asset records can reference instead of a 2D floor-plan pin, enabling metric mounting-height/viewing-distance computation; sits between Indoor Localization and the Sign Inventory Store only if built.

## Core data entities

| Entity | Description | Key relationships |
|---|---|---|
| `Tenant/Organization` | A consulting firm or hospital system; the hard multi-tenancy boundary (schema-level isolation, not row-filtering, given floor-plan/security sensitivity). | Owns `Building`s; scopes all `User`/`Role` access. |
| `Building` → `Floor` → `Space/Room` | The location hierarchy a given tenant's facilities are organized into. | `Space/Room` is the stable location key that `SignObservation`s bind to, whether via Phase 1 manual tap or Phase 2 automated fusion. |
| `Sign` | Persistent physical-object identity — the "same sign" across every audit cycle it's ever observed in. | Has many `SignObservation`s and `SignVersion`s over time. |
| `SignObservation` | One sighting of a sign in one capture session: image, OCR text, classification (Phase 2), confidence, location, timestamp. | Belongs to one `CaptureSession`; many-to-many with `MediaAsset` (one photo can show multiple signs; one sign can appear in multiple assets). |
| `SignVersion` | A materially changed state of a sign (e.g., replaced placard), derived from one or more observations. | Supersedes a prior `SignVersion`, preserving old-evidence/new-evidence linkage for change tracking. |
| `CaptureSession` | One technician's walkthrough: operator, device, date, route/zone. | Owns many `MediaAsset` records. |
| `MediaAsset` | A raw photo/video/frame. | Linked many-to-many to `SignObservation`s. |
| `ComplianceRule` / `ComplianceRuleVersion` *[Phase 2]* | A single requirement (citation, authority tier, jurisdiction scope, effective date, adopted edition, measurable criteria, evidence type required); versioned so a re-audit after a code change can diff "what applied then" vs. "what applies now." | A `ComplianceRuleVersion` generates many `Finding`s. |
| `Finding` *[Phase 2]* | Sign × rule-version × observation, with AI confidence score and evidence pointer (Phase 1 equivalent: the specialist's manually entered finding, with no `ComplianceRuleVersion`/confidence field populated). | Belongs to one `Sign`, one `ComplianceRuleVersion`; generates one `ReviewDecision`. |
| `ReviewDecision` | Reviewer identity/credentials, verdict (concur/override/requires-field-verification), rationale, timestamp. | Belongs to one `Finding`; optionally generates a `RemediationTicket`. |
| `RemediationTicket` *[Phase 2]* | Status, assignee, due date, closure evidence; ideally pushed into the customer's own CMMS rather than duplicated. | Belongs to one `ReviewDecision`. |
| `User`/`Role` | Scoped per tenant, with row-level permissions down to Building/Floor; a distinct "evidence reviewer" role gates raw-media access per the privacy model in Section 11. | Scoped to `Tenant`. |

This mirrors DAM (asset + derivative + metadata), GIS (feature + geometry + attribute versioning), and BIM/CMMS (asset + work-order + location hierarchy) conceptual patterns [ASSUMPTION, architecture lane — standard patterns in comparable domains, no single named product architecture confirmed]. Critically, **the Phase 1 schema is a strict subset of the Phase 2 schema** — no entity is renamed or restructured when B's automation layer is added, only new fields (`confidence`, `ComplianceRuleVersion` linkage, AI-model/version provenance) populate onto entities that already exist. This is the concrete technical expression of the staged recommendation in Section 7.

# 9. Field-capture workflow

The Phase 1 (Concept A) capture workflow, drawing on the capture-workflow research lane:

**Hardware.** Any current smartphone (iOS/Android) — no LiDAR, AR headset, 360° camera, or specialized scanner required [RECOMMENDATION, capture-workflow lane: smartphone is "best default for most hospitals," ubiquitous, low training burden, easiest IT/security review]. Stop-and-shoot still photography, not continuous video, is the default capture mode for Concept A specifically because a human will review every image and the incidental-PHI capture surface of still photos is smaller than continuous video [RECOMMENDATION].

**Structure: escort-defined zones, not calendar time.** Hospitals grant physical access by unit/wing with an escort or badge scope, so the work unit is "this wing, this access window," not "one full floor in one sitting." The app supports start/pause/resume of a zone across multiple visits and merges partial passes into one coverage record [RECOMMENDATION, capture-workflow lane].

**Per-hallway/room loop:**
1. App shows the relevant floor-plan segment with a "last captured" overlay.
2. Technician walks the corridor, photographing each sign of interest (human-identified — Concept A does not auto-detect signs).
3. App timestamps and geo-tags each capture to a *relative* position on the floor plan (never GPS — GPS does not work reliably inside multi-story buildings [FACT, indoor-localization lane]).
4. At each doorway/junction, the app requires an explicit room/space-ID confirmation — voice, barcode/QR on an existing room placard, or tap-to-select on the floor plan — so photos bind to a stable location key. This single control is what Concept A substitutes for the automated VIO/SLAM fusion that Phase 2 will later add, and it eliminates the biggest indoor-localization failure modes (drift, perceptual aliasing in repetitive corridors, elevator tracking loss) by never relying on continuous automated tracking in the first place [RECOMMENDATION, indoor-localization lane].
5. On-device blur/glare scoring flags an immediate, non-blocking "retake" nudge — surfaced as a single unobtrusive prompt, never a hard gate, since blocking flow in a live clinical corridor is unacceptable [FACT/RECOMMENDATION, capture-workflow lane].
6. A single pause/resume control preserves session state, since interruption (carts, gurneys, staff, escort scheduling) is the normal case in a hospital, not an edge case.

**Offline operation.** The app is offline-first by architecture, not merely offline-tolerant: local storage is the sole source of truth during capture, sync is a background enhancement once connectivity returns, and the technician sees an explicit, honest "saved locally / not yet synced" indicator [RECOMMENDATION, capture-workflow lane].

**Privacy in capture.** A hard "no-record" mode/geofence for restricted units (behavioral health, NICU, OR, ED, pharmacy/security) is a capture-app feature from day one, not a later add-on — see Section 11.

**Throughput expectation** [ESTIMATE, capture-workflow lane, pending pilot validation]: roughly one mid-size nursing unit (~20-40 rooms plus corridors) per 60-90 minutes of active walking, excluding escort wait time.

**Evolution to Phase 2 (Concept B).** When detection/localization automation is added, the capture app adds continuous-sweep video/360° capture as an option alongside stop-and-shoot, with on-device best-frame auto-selection and VIO/SLAM tracking fused against the same room-confirmation checkpoints Phase 1 already established — the checkpoint discipline built for Concept A becomes the drift-correction mechanism for Concept B's automated tracking, rather than being replaced by it [RECOMMENDATION].

# 10. Human compliance-review workflow

**Reviewer qualification standard.** [RECOMMENDATION] Every gating mechanism in this package rests on a "qualified human reviewer," so the minimum bar for who may exercise the approve/concur authority in Section 10 needs to be stated explicitly rather than left implicit. Absent a customer-specific contractual requirement, this research proposes the reviewer be at least one of: (a) a CASp (California)/RAS (Texas)-certified accessibility specialist where the jurisdiction recognizes such certification; (b) a licensed architect or engineer with documented ADA/accessibility-code training or continuing-education credit; or (c) an in-house ADA Coordinator or facilities compliance staff member meeting a minimum documented-experience threshold (e.g., a minimum number of years or completed audits). **[OPEN QUESTION]** No AHJ, accreditor, or state licensing body surveyed in this research codifies a single required credential for a "sign-compliance reviewer" specifically — the credential bar in practice is set by each firm's own professional-liability insurance and by individual customer risk tolerance, not by a uniform external standard, and the exact threshold for options (a)–(c) should be settled with real customers (see the Risk Management/Legal-Compliance and ADA Consultant interview blocks in Section 18) before being hard-coded into the product's role model.

**Phase 1 (Concept A) review.** The compliance specialist opens a floor-plan-indexed gallery — photo + confirmed location + OCR'd text (as a search hint only, not a finding) — organized by building/floor/room, replacing the raw-photo-dump-plus-spreadsheet status quo. For each sign, the specialist: identifies which observed condition is a sign of interest, determines the applicable rule stack for that jurisdiction/facility type, judges compliance for every relevant characteristic, and enters a finding and rationale directly into the tool. No AI recommendation exists yet at this stage; the tool's value is purely evidence organization and eliminating transcription labor. The technician and reviewer may be the same person (small engagements) or different roles (firms separating field collection from expert review) [RECOMMENDATION, Concept A].

**Phase 2 (Concept B) review, once added.** The reviewer sees, for each `Finding`: the source image(s) (evidence bundle with capture timestamp and floor-plan coordinate), the exact `ComplianceRuleVersion` cited (citation, authority tier, edition in force on the audit date), the AI's proposed classification/detection and confidence score, and the specific measurable threshold it was compared against — evidence and rule text displayed side by side, per the architecture lane's defensible-record design. The reviewer's available actions:
- **Approve/concur** — the AI finding becomes authoritative, timestamped with reviewer identity and credentials.
- **Override/reject** — reviewer records a decision that disagrees with the AI recommendation, with a written rationale required (especially important on override, since this is the record most likely to be scrutinized under discovery or AHJ dispute).
- **Request rescan/field verification** — the mandatory routing for any characteristic the CV-feasibility lane flags as not reliably automatable from ordinary photo/video: mounting height (no scale reference), LRV contrast (requires a colorimeter), tactile character depth and Braille dot geometry (below LiDAR/photo resolution). These findings are never auto-closed; they generate a targeted rescan/physical-spot-check work item rather than a pass/fail verdict [RECOMMENDATION, CV-feasibility and regulatory lanes].
- **Escalate** — routes ambiguous AHJ-interpretation cases (e.g., "conspicuous placement" under EMTALA, sightline/dead-end judgment calls) to a senior reviewer or the firm's own legal/code-consultant function, since these are expert-judgment calls a rule engine can flag as candidates but not resolve [FACT, CV-feasibility lane].

**Audit trail, preserved identically in both phases** (Phase 1 simply leaves the AI-specific fields empty): rule text/citation and exact version/edition in force on the audit date; the evidence bundle (image, capture timestamp, floor-plan coordinate, measurement method/instrument where applicable); the AI's proposed finding, model/version, and confidence score (Phase 2 only); the reviewer's identity/credentials, decision, and written rationale (mandatory on any override); remediation status and closure evidence (photo of fix, date, responsible party); and full chain-of-custody metadata (who/when captured, processed, reviewed, with unaltered originals retained) [RECOMMENDATION, regulatory and architecture lanes]. This structure is designed to survive Joint Commission survey, DOJ ADA complaint, or litigation discovery without a surveyor or opposing counsel being able to characterize any finding as unsupported inference.

# 11. Privacy, security, and data-governance model

**Recording inside a hospital — baseline risk.** Any camera-based capture tool in a hospital risks incidental PHI exposure (whiteboards, monitors, wristbands, bystanders) even when signage, not patients, is the intended subject. New York-Presbyterian's $2.2M HHS OCR settlement (April 2016) for "virtually unfettered" film-crew access is a directly cautionary, binding-enforcement precedent, not a hypothetical [FACT, business-process lane, hhs.gov]. HIPAA's Privacy Rule generally tolerates *incidental* PHI capture in public/hallway areas where reasonable safeguards apply [FACT, secondary-sourced — paubox.com/hipaatimes.com summarize, not primary HHS text; counsel should verify directly against 45 CFR 164.502(a)(1)(iii) before relying on this conclusion, per the CV-feasibility lane's own caveat], but this tolerance does not extend to restricted units.

**Restricted units.** Behavioral health, NICU, OR, ED, pharmacy, and security areas require explicit capture-exclusion (a hard "no-record" geofence/mode switch) or heightened escort/authorization and redaction discipline, regardless of concept phase [FACT/RECOMMENDATION, capture-workflow lane].

**Access controls.** Raw photo/video access should be RBAC-gated to a narrow "evidence reviewer" role from Phase 1 onward; broader roles (report viewers, remediation trackers, cross-building aggregation) see only cropped sign-region derivatives and extracted attributes, never raw unredacted media [RECOMMENDATION, architecture lane]. Floor plans and camera footage of a hospital's physical layout are themselves a security asset and must be access-controlled independently of the compliance use case — this becomes a materially larger concern if the Phase 3 spatial-twin layer is ever added, since a persistent point cloud is a durable record of sightlines and secured-area geometry that outlives any single audit.

**Redaction.** On-device pre-redaction of incidentally captured faces and PHI-bearing surfaces before any cloud transmission is a reasonable safeguard [RECOMMENDATION] — no vendor product was found in this research implementing this specifically for the sign-audit use case, so it should be treated as a differentiator to build, not an assumed off-the-shelf capability. Redaction matters more once Phase 2 introduces continuous video capture than it does for Phase 1's stop-and-shoot stills, where the operator already exercises deliberate framing control.

**Retention and chain of custody.** Retention limits on raw media should be distinct from (and shorter than) retention on the derived compliance record itself, since the compliance record (finding, rule version, reviewer decision, remediation status) is the durable business asset while raw media is primarily evidentiary support for a bounded audit cycle [RECOMMENDATION, architecture lane]. Every access to raw media should be audit-logged. Unaltered original media should be retained (not just derivatives) to support the defensible-record requirement in Section 10, but access to those originals is exactly what the evidence-reviewer RBAC role above must gate.

**Data ownership.** The building owner (hospital, or the consulting firm's client) should own the sign inventory, floor-plan associations, and evidence photos as their business record — mirroring the Matterport Platform Subscription Agreement template, where the customer owns "Customer Data" and the deliverable while the vendor retains only rights to its own underlying technology [FACT, commercial-model lane, matterport.com/legal]. The vendor's license should be limited to using de-identified/aggregate detections to improve its models, with explicit contractual and technical carve-outs for any incidentally captured PHI (redaction before persistence, BAA availability for any subprocessor touching PHI-adjacent media).

**General security posture** (applies from Phase 1): encryption at rest/in transit, SOC 2 Type II readiness, BAA availability, audit logging of every raw-media access, and least-privilege access to full floor plans [RECOMMENDATION, architecture lane]. None of this is deferrable to Phase 2 — a consulting firm's own client contracts and hospital-access agreements will require it before the very first pilot engagement.

# 12. Competitive landscape

**Closest direct competitor: Reconstruct Inc. — "AI Automated Inspector"** [FACT, reconstructinc.com/automatedinspector.ai]. A funded ($27.4M raised, founded 2016) construction reality-capture vendor that has extended its 360°-camera/photogrammetry platform into hospital compliance: captures via 360 cameras/smartphones/drones, classifies fire-safety components, medical equipment, and ADA/accessibility elements with ML, builds a 3D map, and generates compliance reports referencing Joint Commission, CMS CoPs, NFPA 101/99, and ADA. This is the single most literal existing analog to the product vision. **Differentiation:** Reconstruct's public marketing is asset/equipment-centric across the whole facility, not shown to specialize in signage text/symbol/pictogram/tactile-Braille reading, sign-specific dedup, or a signage-specific jurisdiction-resolved rule library. The recommended product's moat is exactly that narrow specialization, executed first at low automation risk (Concept A) rather than attempting the full facility-wide AI classification Reconstruct already markets.

**Workflow/records substitute and possible integration partner: Ruya** [FACT, ruyacompliance.com]. Mobile-first hospital compliance workflow platform (asset/vendor directory, live floor-plan sync in PDF/DWG/Revit) serving health systems with 200M+ sq ft under management. No AI/CV capture advertised — it is a records/workflow layer, not a detection engine. **Differentiation/positioning:** a natural downstream integration target (feed detected signs and findings into its inspection/asset directory) rather than a competitor, since it does not attempt what this product does upstream.

**Rules-engine integration candidate: firecodes.ai** [FACT, firecodes.ai]. AI tool for querying state-adopted NFPA 101/99/25/72 code text; explicitly does not touch physical signage or capture. **Differentiation/positioning:** a potential API partner for the code-lookup side of the jurisdiction-resolved rules engine (Section 8), not a competitor.

**Capability analogs validating the CV-plus-human-review pattern:** OpenSpace and Buildots [FACT, openspace.ai; forconstructionpros.com] pair panoramic/hardhat-camera capture with BIM comparison to auto-detect installed components, with OpenSpace Track explicitly requiring human review before a "verified" report ships — the same AI-plus-qualified-reviewer pattern this recommendation already assumes, deployed at scale (95,000+ projects). Trax Retail and Simbe Robotics [FACT, traxretail.com; simberobotics.com] prove CV-catalog-matching (detect an object, match against a known-type catalog, flag anomalies) works commercially at ~99% SKU accuracy — directly analogous to "detect a sign, match against a sign-type catalog," though in a visually easier retail environment than hospital corridors. Blyncsy [FACT, blyncsy.com] is the most literal "AI + video + sign detection + classification + change-tracking" precedent available today — but for outdoor DOT road signage, a different taxonomy, lighting regime, and density than indoor hospital wayfinding/ADA signage. **Differentiation:** none of these four operate in the indoor-hospital-signage space; they establish that the individual techniques work, not that anyone has solved this specific domain.

**Incumbent manual-consultant substitutes:** Bureau Veritas North America [FACT, bvna.com] runs an in-house national ADA team plus "Facility Equipment Inventory & Tagging" — the exact manual workflow this product displaces, from a well-capitalized incumbent with existing hospital relationships. Kimley-Horn [FACT, kimley-horn.com] built its own proprietary tablet/GPS field-data tool ("KADA") for municipal ADA transition plans, showing incumbents are already investing in technology to defend the manual-audit relationship rather than standing still. Smaller boutiques (ADA Compliance Network, ADA Compliance Pros, Direct Access USA, PFI Sign Solutions, DDS International) remain fully manual/tablet-checklist-based [FACT] — this is the segment most exposed to displacement by Concept A alone, and the easiest initial wedge since these firms have the least proprietary tooling of their own to defend.

**Capture-substrate partners or build-vs-buy alternatives:** Matterport (Professional ~$58/mo; Enterprise custom) and NavVis (VLX hardware ~$30k+, service-bureau rates ~$0.20-$0.70/sq ft with a 1.6x hospital-complexity multiplier) [FACT/ESTIMATE, matterport.com; thefuture3d.com] perform capture/visualization but no sign-specific detection, classification, or compliance evaluation. **Differentiation/positioning:** these are integration partners or a build-vs-buy decision point if the Phase 3 spatial-twin layer is ever pursued — ingesting their point-cloud output rather than building in-house LiDAR capture trades margin for speed to market [RECOMMENDATION, commercial-model lane].

**Eventual system-of-record targets:** Accruent (600+ healthcare orgs on its CMMS), Planon, and Eptura [FACT, accruent.com; infraspeak.com] already sit inside hospital facilities departments running 12-24-month, six-to-seven-figure IWMS deployments; none does sign-specific AI capture. **Differentiation/positioning:** the recommended product should push structured records (location, condition, violation, remediation) into whichever CMMS the hospital or consulting firm already runs, not compete with these platforms [RECOMMENDATION, architecture and commercial-model lanes].

**Net competitive positioning for the recommended (Concept A→B) product:** no vendor found in this research combines (a) a signage-specific taxonomy and jurisdiction-resolved, versioned compliance rule library, (b) a low-risk, quickly-adoptable Phase 1 product that matches how ADA consulting firms already buy today, and (c) an explicit staged path to AI-assisted detection once real-world evidence justifies it. Reconstruct is the nearest threat by resources and market position but is not shown to have (a); the incumbents have distribution and trust but not (a) or (c); the capture-substrate and CMMS vendors are potential partners, not competitors, for this specific niche.

# 13. Business and pricing hypotheses

**Hypothesis for the recommended Phase 1 (Concept A) product:** price as a **per-project or per-building seat/subscription license sold to consulting firms**, not a per-sign or per-scan usage fee. [RECOMMENDATION] This follows directly from two findings in the commercial-model lane: (1) one-time project audits are how the market buys today, so a per-project license (one-time or annual for firms running repeat engagements) requires no category-education cost; and (2) usage-based pricing (per-scan/per-sign) is a documented SaaS pricing failure mode here specifically because it discourages the very re-audits that keep an inventory current — exactly the behavior this product needs to encourage, since a stale inventory is one of the core failure modes the business-process lane identified in the existing manual process (the BlueDAG precedent: "70% of municipalities have no ADA transition plan, or one decades out of date"). An annual-subscription tier for firms running many engagements captures expansion revenue without penalizing frequent re-scanning.

**Hypothesis for the Phase 2 (Concept B) evolution, to be validated once B's pilot criteria are met:** transition to the **two-phase hybrid identified across the commercial-model lane for every concept in this space** — (1) a one-time, per-square-foot-priced managed-service capture/build engagement (vendor-trained technicians, not hospital/firm staff, since untrained first-pass capture carries real PHI/restricted-unit risk), anchored to reality-capture market comps of roughly $0.20-$2.10/sq ft depending on complexity [ESTIMATE, thefuture3d.com], followed by (2) a per-building/per-sq-ft **annual compliance-monitoring subscription** covering hosting, change-detection re-scans, and reviewer workflow — mirroring the IWMS/CMMS budget line hospitals already carry (Accruent-class contracts). Self-serve capture by consulting-firm or hospital staff becomes a viable year-2+ option once accuracy and redaction QA are proven in the field, lowering marginal cost without repeating the privacy risk of untrained first-time capture.

**Data ownership hypothesis (applies from Phase 1, not deferred to Phase 2):** the building owner or consulting firm's client owns the sign inventory, floor-plan associations, and evidence photos as their own business record — the Matterport Platform Subscription Agreement template (customer owns "Customer Data" and the deliverable; vendor retains only its own underlying technology) is the right one to mirror [FACT, commercial-model lane]. This is the correct default even before any ML model exists to train, because it is what makes the subscription sticky (the customer is paying to retain access to *their own* asset) and because consulting firms will resist any vendor contract that appears to claim ownership of their client audit deliverables — a Phase 1 sales-cycle risk, not just a Phase 2 one. The vendor's license should be limited to de-identified/aggregate use for future model improvement, established contractually in Phase 1 even though there is no model to improve yet, so the Phase 2 transition does not require renegotiating data rights with an already-onboarded customer base.

**Why not price on the spatial-data asset itself (Concept C's implied model) at this stage:** the commercial-model lane's strongest argument for pricing the *spatial capture* itself (rather than compliance access) rests on reuse cases — space planning, emergency response, renovation tracking — that are proven in adjacent domains (Matterport's own marketing; the ATI Restoration ~$110k/year case study) but unvalidated for this product's actual first buyer (a consulting firm, not a facilities/real-estate team that would value the twin for its own sake). This reuse-based pricing hypothesis should be revisited only if/when a Concept-C-style customer (Facilities/Real Estate leadership) is pursued as a distinct second segment, not applied to the recommended Phase 1/2 product.

**Open pricing questions not resolved by available sources** [OPEN, commercial-model lane]: no vendor in this space publicly discloses whether it offers automated on-device PHI redaction as a priced feature or a bundled safeguard; and no vendor's public materials address liability allocation (vendor vs. owner) if an AI-flagged "compliant" finding is later found non-compliant during a Joint Commission or AHJ inspection — this should be resolved contractually (indemnification/limitation-of-liability terms) before Phase 2 pricing is finalized, since Phase 2 is the first point at which the product itself issues a compliance recommendation. This is precisely the question the new Risk Management/Legal-Compliance interview block in Section 18 is designed to surface directly with customers, rather than leaving it as an internal guess.

# 15. Pilot design with measurable success criteria

**[RECOMMENDATION]** Run a single-site pilot rather than a multi-building rollout, so every measured outcome can be checked against a same-building manual ground truth rather than aggregated across dissimilar buildings.

**Site:** One hospital wing or one outpatient/ambulatory clinic building (150–400 signs, per the scale implied in Concept B's own pilot design), preferably an administrative or outpatient area to minimize restricted-unit/PHI exposure during a first pilot; if only inpatient space is available, exclude behavioral health/NICU/OR/ED/pharmacy/security units from the pilot's capture scope entirely rather than attempting redaction-under-fire on a first pass. **[RECOMMENDATION]**

**Duration:** 4–6 weeks total — 1–2 weeks capture (escort-scoped, zone-by-zone per the field-usability lane's workflow model), 1–2 weeks processing, 1–2 weeks reviewer adjudication and comparison analysis.

**What's captured:** Smartphone photo/video walkthrough using the tap-on-floor-plan/room-placard-confirmation workflow (Concept A/B hybrid), run by one or two trained technicians. In parallel, an independent consultant/technician performs a fully manual audit (photo → spreadsheet → floor plan) of the *same* wing, producing the ground-truth sign count, location list, and a subset of physically re-measured attributes (mounting height via tape measure, contrast via colorimeter on a sample) to anchor the "requires field verification" comparison. A second, lighter capture pass is scheduled 2–4 weeks after the first, during which 10–15 signs are deliberately moved, replaced, or removed by the site team (with facilities' knowledge) to generate ground truth for change detection.

**What's measured**, mapped one-to-one against the required criteria list, with **[ESTIMATE]**-labeled proposed targets to be validated (not assumed) by the pilot itself:

1. **Sign-detection recall and precision** — against the manual ground-truth count. Target: ≥90% recall, ≥85% precision. [ESTIMATE]
2. **Duplicate-detection accuracy** — false-merge rate (two distinct signs collapsed into one) and false-split rate (one sign counted twice). Target: false-merge <5%, false-split <10%, both requiring human confirmation regardless (per Concept A/B's assistive-not-autonomous dedup design). [ESTIMATE]
3. **OCR and classification accuracy** — measured separately for clean printed text vs. stylized/oblique text, since the CV-feasibility lane documents a real accuracy cliff between the two (~98% vs. ~62–80%). Target: ≥90% character accuracy on clean text, report (don't gate on) stylized-text accuracy as a descriptive baseline. [ESTIMATE]
4. **Indoor location accuracy** — median linear position error of each detected sign vs. its manually surveyed true position on the floor plan. Target: median error ≤3 m / within one room of ground truth. [ESTIMATE]
5. **% of signs correctly assigned to the right room or doorway** — the room/space key attached to each sign observation, verified against the manual ground truth. Target: ≥80%, matching Concept B's own validating threshold. [ESTIMATE]
6. **Capture time per floor / per square foot** — technician walk-and-shoot duration (tool-assisted) vs. the parallel manual audit's walk-and-shoot duration for the same wing, normalized per 1,000 sq ft. Target: ≤50–70% of manual capture time. [ESTIMATE]
7. **Human review time saved** — compliance specialist hours to produce a finalized, floor-plan-indexed finding set, tool-assisted vs. fully manual, for a matched subset of signs. Target: ≥40% reduction, matching Concept B's validating threshold (Concept A alone targets ≥30%). [ESTIMATE]
8. **Missed-sign rate** — % of ground-truth signs (from the manual audit) never surfaced anywhere in the tool's output (missed by the technician, the detector, or both — reported as a single end-to-end figure since a missed sign is a missed sign regardless of which stage failed). Target: <10%. [ESTIMATE]
9. **False compliance finding rate** — restricted to characteristics the tool is attempting to auto-flag (presence checks, character style, layout), comparing AI-proposed finding vs. the reviewing specialist's independent judgment on the same evidence. Target: <10% disagreement in either direction (false-compliant or false-violation). [ESTIMATE]
10. **% of compliance attributes requiring manual measurement** — a descriptive, not pass/fail, metric: track what fraction of the full applicable-characteristic checklist per sign type gets routed "requires field verification" (mounting height, LRV contrast, tactile depth, Braille dot geometry, per the CV-feasibility lane's determination that none of these are reliably photo-derivable). Expected range to report against: roughly 25–40% of characteristics per accessible sign, given 4–6 non-automatable characteristics out of a typical 10–15-item ADA signage checklist. [ESTIMATE]
11. **Successful change detection across repeat surveys** — using the deliberately altered 10–15 signs from the second capture pass, measure the % correctly flagged as changed/moved/removed, and separately measure the false-change flag rate among the remaining untouched signs. Target: ≥80% correct-change detection, <10% false-change flags on unmoved signs. [ESTIMATE]

**Validating result (overall):** the pilot validates moving into MVP-scope automation if criteria 1, 5, and 7 clear their thresholds simultaneously — detection is accurate enough, location assignment is trustworthy enough, and reviewers actually save time — mirroring Concept B's own stated validating bar. **Killing/pivot result:** if criterion 5 (room/doorway assignment) falls materially below 60%, per Concept B's own kill signal, localization is not trustworthy at the granularity the compliance record needs and the roadmap should hold at Concept A automation levels (manual/assisted tagging only) until localization is separately re-engineered.

# 16. Major assumptions and experiments needed

**Technical assumptions**

- **A general-purpose or zero-shot detector can find "signs" in hospital corridors without a purpose-built training dataset.** No purpose-built building-signage detection dataset exists publicly — only road-sign datasets (CV-feasibility lane). **[ASSUMPTION]** Experiment: the Phase 1 PoC spike (Section 14) running an off-the-shelf/zero-shot detector against real institutional signage photos, measured before any custom-dataset investment is committed.
- **VIO+checkpoint fusion holds room/door-level accuracy across a real multi-floor hospital, including elevator transits.** No single method in the indoor-localization lane is independently trustworthy at that granularity; the layered approach is a reasoned recommendation, not a validated result. **[ASSUMPTION]** Experiment: the Phase 1 localization spike explicitly including at least one elevator/stairwell transit and one long, visually repetitive corridor (both documented failure modes).
- **Sign dedup is tractable as landmark re-observation/SLAM loop-closure, not pedestrian-style re-identification.** No paper specifically validates this mapping for signage. **[ASSUMPTION]** Experiment: pilot criterion 2 (walk the same corridor from multiple angles/times, measure false-merge/false-split rates directly).
- **OCR/VLM classification is accurate enough on real (aged, damaged, stylized, obliquely-photographed) placards, not just clean lab-benchmark text.** Standard OCR benchmarks (ICDAR, COCO-Text, TextOCR) skew toward large, legible text and under-represent the small, low-contrast, oblique conditions typical of hospital room-ID plaques (CV-feasibility lane). **[ASSUMPTION]** Experiment: pilot criterion 3, explicitly segmented by clean vs. stylized text, not a single blended accuracy number.
- **Change detection across repeat indoor surveys is achievable with acceptable false-positive/negative rates.** No dedicated literature was located for this specific problem. **[ASSUMPTION]** Experiment: pilot criterion 11's deliberate-alteration second pass.

**Regulatory/legal assumptions**

- **No AHJ or accreditor currently accepts fully automated sign-compliance determinations, and none is likely to soon.** This is an absence-of-evidence finding, not a confirmed negative. **[ASSUMPTION]** Experiment: direct interview with a compliance consultant and/or DOJ ADA technical-assistance contact (Section 18) before this assumption is relied upon to justify *any* auto-closed finding, however narrow.
- **A jurisdiction-resolver rules-as-data model (mapping address + facility type + CMS provider number to the applicable federal/model-code/state/local/accreditation stack) is buildable and maintainable without unmanageable legal-review overhead.** No off-the-shelf analog exists (product-architecture lane). **[ASSUMPTION]** Experiment: attempt to fully author the rule library for the single pilot building's actual jurisdiction end-to-end, and measure legal/code-consultant hours consumed, before assuming this scales linearly to additional jurisdictions.
- **The ~65–70% LRV contrast figure this package cites (Section 4.2, Section 5) as the practical accessibility-industry convention for the ADA's contrast requirement, and other secondary-sourced numeric figures used elsewhere in this package, are accurate representations of binding text**, not vendor rounding or informal convention. That figure is drawn from industry/vendor secondary sources (signs.org, latitudesignage.com) summarizing Access Board guidance, not from this research team's own line-by-line reading of the primary federal text (36 CFR Part 1191 and the incorporated 2010 ADA Standards §703.5.1), so it should be treated as directionally correct but numerically unconfirmed. **[ASSUMPTION]** Experiment: direct primary-source review (or compliance-counsel review) of the actual federal text and any state-amended contrast language before encoding any numeric LRV threshold — 65–70% or otherwise — as a hard automated rule anywhere in the product.

**Commercial assumptions**

- **Third-party ADA/accessibility consulting firms are the right first customer and distribution channel**, ahead of a direct hospital sale. **[RECOMMENDATION, business-process lane]** treated here as an assumption requiring validation. Experiment: structured interviews/sales conversations with 3–5 consulting firms (Section 18 questions) testing actual willingness to pay and workflow fit, not just directional interest.
- **A managed-service capture model (vendor-trained technicians) is necessary because untrained hospital staff cannot reliably execute correct capture/tagging/redaction technique on a first pass.** **[ASSUMPTION, commercial-model lane]** Experiment: run the pilot with both a trained technician and, on a small matched subset, an untrained hospital staff volunteer, and directly compare location-tagging error rates and redaction compliance.
- **A one-time per-sq-ft capture engagement followed by a per-building annual subscription is what the market will actually pay**, versus a pure one-time project fee (the market's current norm) or usage-based pricing. **[ASSUMPTION]** Experiment: test at least two pricing structures in parallel pricing conversations during pilot-partner recruitment, not just present one model as a foregone conclusion.
- **The reusable spatial-capture asset (space planning, emergency response, renovation tracking) creates enough incremental willingness-to-pay to justify Concept C's premium cost**, independent of compliance value alone. **[ASSUMPTION, commercial-model lane]** Experiment: the structured facilities-stakeholder interview specified in Concept C's own pilot design, asking for a concrete, priced use case — not a generic "that sounds useful" response.

**Privacy assumption**

- **On-device redaction (face/screen/whiteboard blurring) before any cloud transmission is a practically achievable and legally sufficient safeguard for incidental PHI capture.** No vendor in this space was found advertising this specifically implemented (commercial-model lane). **[ASSUMPTION]** Experiment: build and test a minimal redaction pipeline during the Phase 1 PoC in a controlled setting, and have it assessed by a privacy/compliance reviewer (ideally hospital privacy officer or outside counsel) against a "reasonable safeguards, minimum necessary" HIPAA standard — not merely assumed sufficient because a redaction step exists.

# 18. Open questions requiring customer or compliance-expert interviews

**Hospital Facilities Director / VP of Facilities**
- How many signs does your facility actually have (by building/floor), and does any inventory — even an outdated spreadsheet — currently exist?
- What CMMS/IWMS platform do you run today, and would you want sign-compliance findings and work orders pushed into it, or kept in a separate system?
- What escort/access rules apply to which units (behavioral health, NICU, OR, ED, pharmacy/security), and realistically how far in advance do you need to schedule access to each?
- Do you have an existing BIM or CAD floor plan, and how current is it relative to as-built reality after your most recent renovations?
- Who owns the ADA transition plan/self-evaluation documentation internally, and how does compliance status get communicated across facilities, risk management, and legal today?
- What would make you trust an AI-proposed finding enough to act on it without independently re-verifying every single one yourself?
- What's your budget cycle and typical spend for signage remediation, and does an annual subscription fit that cycle better or worse than a one-time project fee?
- Would your IT/security team require a formal security review, a BAA, or specific network segmentation before any third-party capture app could run on hospital devices or Wi-Fi?

**Third-party ADA/Accessibility Compliance Consultant**
- Walk me through your actual field-survey process today, start to finish — how many signs per hour or per day do you typically capture, and where does the real bottleneck sit?

- What proportion of your findings actually require a physical measurement tool (tape measure, colorimeter, calipers) versus a purely visual judgment call?

# 20. Concise recommendation

Build **Concept A (Low-Risk Assisted Audit)** first, as **Phase 1** of the roadmap in Section 14, sold into third-party ADA/accessibility consulting firms — the segment already performing this exact manual workflow as billable-hour labor. It requires no custom-trained detector, no localization fusion, and no compliance rules engine, carries no AI-misjudgment liability, and directly attacks the two most-cited, unbenchmarked pain points: manual photo-to-floor-plan matching and file-based, non-versioned handoffs. Run the single-building pilot in Section 15 alongside this build to generate real evidence — not assumptions — on detection feasibility, localization accuracy, and reviewer time savings.

**Defer** Concept B's automated detection/dedup/rules-engine layer until that pilot evidence justifies it (per Section 14's phased gate), and defer **Concept C**'s spatial-digital-twin entirely — it is the highest-cost, highest-privacy-exposure, slowest-to-sell option, and defensible only after a facilities stakeholder has named a concrete, priced non-compliance use case, which no interview in this research corpus has yet confirmed. Also defer a Joint Commission/life-safety-led sales motion: that stakeholder's signage interest is narrower and a different buyer than ADA room-signage, and would undersell the product's core value on a slower enterprise sales cycle. Treat Concept A's evidence corpus and design-partner relationships as the asset that earns the right to build Concept B, not as the final product.

Before finalizing liability language, reviewer-qualification requirements, or Phase 2 pricing, close the two interview gaps this revision adds in Section 18 (Risk Management/Legal-Compliance; a Life Safety Officer voice distinct from the Joint Commission survey-readiness lead) — both bear directly on open items already flagged in Sections 10, 13, and 17 (risk #6).
